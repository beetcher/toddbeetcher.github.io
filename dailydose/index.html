A Daily Dose of
Cognitive Flow

Building the judgment our children will need in an AI-infused world.

Parents are being asked to prepare their children for a world none of us can describe particularly well.

We know artificial intelligence will be deeply infused into it. We know many of the tasks people have historically been paid, rewarded, and recognized for performing will increasingly be done with or by machines. We know the applications our children use, the jobs they hold, and perhaps even the occupations they enter will change substantially between now and adulthood. Yet when parents reasonably ask, What is one important thing I can help my child develop now that will matter in the world they're entering?, the answers quickly become overwhelming.

Teach them to code. Teach them to prompt. Give them AI literacy. Emphasize STEM. Teach creativity, resilience, entrepreneurship, adaptability, critical thinking.

All of those may matter. But I want to focus on one foundational human capability that appears repeatedly when people talk seriously about the future of human contribution in an AI-infused world: judgment.

There is a troubling dichotomy emerging around that capability. Just as judgment may become increasingly important to our children's ability to remain meaningful contributors to society, many of the conditions through which children historically developed the underlying capacities for judgment may be becoming less readily available to them, while comparatively little attention is being paid to deliberately putting those conditions back.

Most adults know the experience I am going to describe because at some point we have experienced it ourselves. We have stared at a difficult problem long enough for something to happen. We have taken long walks without answers immediately available to us. We have built things, fixed things, argued with ourselves, become bored, struggled with problems, and spent periods of time alone with our own thoughts because there simply wasn't another option. We didn't necessarily call any of that cognitive development. Some of it was simply the environment in which we grew up.

Our children inhabit a different environment, filled with interruption and constant switching. We have made immediate stimulation and immediate answers ubiquitous, creating extraordinary mechanisms for satisfying curiosity without requiring someone to remain curious for very long. Now AI offers something even more powerful: access to seemingly limitless intelligence and knowledge without necessarily requiring the person asking the question to perform much of the synthesis themselves.

At precisely the moment when our children may need to become better at thinking deeply, connecting disparate experiences, synthesizing information, forming independent conclusions, and exercising judgment, they may have fewer natural opportunities to practice doing it—and we may be spending less time deliberately cultivating the capability.

That is the dichotomy I want to explore. This isn't a paper about getting children off their phones, nor is it an argument against AI. It isn't a parenting manual or a prescription for changing schools. It is an attempt to identify something we should begin paying much closer attention to: whether our children are getting regular opportunities to enter sustained periods of generative thought, whether they are becoming familiar and comfortable with that cognitive state, and whether they are learning to remain the authors of what emerges from it.

I have come to think of that experience as Generative Cognitive Flow, and I believe its proactive and persistent pursuit may become increasingly important to our children's ability to develop the judgment they will need to remain meaningful contributors in an AI-infused society.

Why Judgment?

Judgment can sound like an abstract executive skill until you imagine the world our children will actually inhabit.

Consider a plumber twenty years from now. There is no reason to believe that plumber won't have access to extraordinarily capable intelligence. An AI system may know the history of a building, analyze images, retrieve applicable codes, compare previous repairs, identify probable causes of a failure, predict the remaining life of components, recommend alternatives, estimate costs, and generate a repair plan.

But someone still needs to stand in the building and ask: Does this make sense here? What is the system missing? Is that really the problem? Which recommendation applies to this situation? What are the consequences if it's wrong? What should we actually do?

That is judgment.

The same thing will happen in medicine, engineering, education, law, construction, finance, design, manufacturing, management, entrepreneurship, and thousands of occupations we cannot yet anticipate. This isn't an "AI worker" capability or an elite knowledge-worker capability. Increasingly, everyone will interact in some form with machine intelligence and machine-generated knowledge. The domain will change, but the human requirement will remain recognizable: our children will need to engage with that intelligence safely, successfully, and soberly.

Artificial intelligence can provide an answer. A human still needs the capacity to form a judgment about the answer.

This connects to a broader concern I explored in Automating Away Our Children's Satisfaction. As technology becomes capable of performing more of the units of work for which humans have historically received recognition and value, the remaining human contribution doesn't simply disappear. It moves. The ability to produce a routine artifact becomes less scarce, while deciding what ought to be produced, what matters, what fits, what should be trusted, how pieces relate, and what should happen next becomes increasingly consequential.

That doesn't mean judgment is the only human capability that will matter. It means that telling our children they will need judgment is easy. Explaining where judgment comes from is considerably harder.

So if judgment is what we need, how do we build judgment?

Where Does Judgment Come From?

There are extensive bodies of work around judgment, reasoning, critical thinking, decision-making, creativity, expertise, metacognition, and learning. I am not suggesting that judgment has a single accepted definition or that one cognitive mechanism explains all of it. I am interested in something more practical.

Think about what happens when someone exercises meaningful judgment. They bring knowledge and experience to the problem, but neither alone is enough. They may simultaneously be considering observations, patterns, context, values, competing possibilities, uncertainty, consequences, and things they learned in situations that initially appear unrelated to the one in front of them.

Somehow they bring those things together and create a result. They synthesize.

As a useful compression, rather than a scientific definition, judgment is synthesis. It is the ability to bring together independent thoughts, learnings, experiences, observations, and context and derive a new result from them.

That result might be an opinion, a concept, a new idea, an interpretation, a decision, a recommendation, a rejection, a hypothesis, or a course of action. Whatever its form, it was not merely retrieved. The person created something from the combination of what they know, what they have experienced, what they are observing, and what they are thinking now.

They are creating their own result.

This is why judgment and creativity exist in such close proximity. Both require us to form relationships among things that may previously have been independent and produce something that did not exist in precisely that form before. In creativity, we may call that result an idea. In judgment, we may call it a conclusion, interpretation, recommendation, or decision. The underlying act of synthesis is closely related.

It would be too strong to say that every act of judgment requires a formal state of cognitive flow, and that is not my claim. My proposition is narrower: the capacities we exercise during sustained periods of generative synthesis appear closely related to the capacities we require when exercising sophisticated judgment. If we want children who can independently synthesize thoughts, learnings, and experiences into new results, then the cognitive states in which they repeatedly practice doing that deserve our attention.

And that brings us to a cognitive experience almost everyone has had, even if they have never had a name for it.

You Know It When It Happens

Maybe it happens while you're walking, driving, writing, building something, standing at a whiteboard, working through a difficult problem, or having an unusually good conversation.

Sometimes it is quiet. Someone asks you a meaningful question and you don't immediately answer. You look away. You stare at the wall. The other person can almost see you thinking. You are holding the question, retrieving experiences, testing possible answers, making and discarding connections, and trying to create a result before you speak.

At other times, the state becomes much more generative and obvious. You begin with one thought. It reminds you of something else. An experience from years ago suddenly becomes relevant to a problem you're thinking about today. Something you learned last week collides with something someone told you this morning. A relationship you hadn't previously noticed becomes obvious, which creates another relationship, which creates another.

And then something peculiar happens.

Connections begin appearing faster than you can verbalize them.

That is one of the clearest ways I recognize that I have moved into a deeper form of cognitive flow. If you're talking to someone, you may start spewing the ideas out so quickly that the other person has trouble following you. You haven't necessarily lost the thread; quite the opposite. In your own mind the connections are forming so rapidly that speech has become the bottleneck. Another thought is arriving before you've finished verbalizing the previous one, and you can't get the ideas out fast enough because you're afraid you're going to lose what you're thinking about.

I've experienced this at whiteboards countless times. I've experienced it while walking and in conversation. Sometimes I get to the other end of it and essentially wonder, Where the hell did that come from?

The answer is not that someone gave me the thought. My own cognition began connecting things rapidly enough that something new emerged from them. That is the particular experience I am trying to capture with the term Generative Cognitive Flow.

I don't think of cognitive flow as a binary state that is either on or off. There appear to be different depths and intensities, much as we experience different depths of sleep. At one level, it may simply look like the person staring back at you and thinking hard before answering a difficult question. At another, someone may become deeply immersed in a problem for an extended period. At its most visibly generative, connections may begin forming faster than the person can verbalize them. The common characteristic I am interested in is not merely concentration. It is sustained cognition becoming increasingly capable of generation and synthesis.

The terminology matters because "flow" already has an established history in psychology. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi famously used the term to describe states of deep absorption characterized by intense concentration and changes in subjective experience. Generative Cognitive Flow, as I am using the term here, isn't intended to replace that established concept. I am interested specifically in its generative characteristic: sustained cognition in which connections form, previously separate experiences combine, relationships become visible, and new thoughts emerge.

Once you've experienced it, you recognize it. The question is whether we recognize its developmental importance.

Focus Isn't the Destination

We talk constantly about children needing to focus. We're right, but I think we're describing only part of the problem.

Removing a phone from a child's hand doesn't create deep thought. A quiet classroom doesn't guarantee synthesis. Sitting still isn't the same as thinking deeply.

Removing distractions creates the conditions for cognitive flow. It is not cognitive flow.

Focus isn't the destination.

The more interesting question is: What does a child do with sustained cognitive attention once they have it?

Can they stay with a difficult question after the obvious answer fails? Can they pull something they learned months ago into something they're encountering today? Can they tolerate uncertainty long enough to explore competing explanations? Can they discover relationships that weren't initially apparent? Can they reject their first conclusion and keep thinking? Can they generate rather than merely retrieve? And do they know the peculiar satisfaction that comes from struggling with something and suddenly realizing, I figured something out?

Those experiences matter because judgment isn't something we should treat as a mysterious attribute that people either have or don't. People will differ in their natural abilities, but many ingredients associated with judgment—knowledge, experience, sustained attention, critical thinking, metacognition, synthesis, and learning to navigate uncertainty—are capacities that can develop.

There is substantial existing work across cognitive psychology and education examining these underlying capacities, including attention, executive function, metacognition, creativity, critical thinking, and the ability to transfer learning from one context to another. I am not attempting to collapse that literature into a new theory. I am asking a simpler developmental question: if sophisticated judgment depends in meaningful part upon our ability to sustain attention, connect experiences, synthesize information, and generate independent conclusions, shouldn't children have repeated opportunities to exercise those capabilities?

Perhaps we should begin thinking not only about whether our children can focus, but whether they regularly get to do something generative with their focus.

A Daily Dose of Cognitive Flow

I don't live in cognitive flow all day. I wouldn't want to. Deep generative thinking can be exhausting. There are different depths and modes of cognition just as there are different states of sleep, and there is no reason to assume that more is always better.

The developmental question is not continuous flow. It is repeated access.

What happens when children regularly experience sustained periods in which their cognition becomes generative? Do they become more comfortable staying with difficult questions and recognizing the conditions under which they think well? Do they become better at noticing their own thinking and practicing synthesis? Might they eventually find comfort, satisfaction, perhaps even joy in entering that state?

The developmental aspiration isn't merely to create children who can tolerate deep thinking when somebody forces them to do it. It is to help create young people for whom sustained generative cognition becomes a familiar human state—something they recognize, know they are capable of entering, and perhaps eventually enjoy because they associate it with discovery, creation, and the satisfaction of producing something that is authentically theirs.

Those are partly empirical questions. I am not claiming that cognitive science has established a prescribed dose of Generative Cognitive Flow that produces judgment, nor that a child who spends a certain number of minutes each day thinking deeply will produce some predictable developmental result.

So when I use the phrase a daily dose of cognitive flow, I mean it as a developmental aspiration, not a clinical dosage recommendation: regular, protected opportunities for sustained generative thought.

Parents, educators, mentors, and institutions can engage in the proactive pursuit of cognitive flow by deliberately looking for environments in which those experiences are possible and treating them as developmentally valuable. The longer-term aspiration is that a child who becomes familiar with the experience eventually develops their own persistent pursuit of cognitive flow—an understanding that deep generative thought is not something unpleasant to escape from as quickly as possible, but a productive and satisfying cognitive state worth returning to throughout life.

That would matter in any era. AI makes it considerably more complicated.

AI Can End the Thinking—or Extend It

Imagine a child encounters a difficult question. For most of human history, there was friction between the question and the answer. The child might have had to remember something, ask someone, read, experiment, make a mistake, stare at the problem, become frustrated, walk away, come back, or connect it to something they already understood. The answer didn't always arrive quickly enough to prevent thinking from occurring.

Today the path can become:

Question → AI → answer.

That is extraordinarily powerful. It can also allow the machine to perform the synthesis before the child has meaningfully attempted it. The child receives an artifact, may become more productive, and may even learn something. But productivity and cognitive development aren't necessarily the same thing, and possession of an artifact is not the same as having authentically generated the thinking the artifact represents.

There is, however, another way to engage with AI, and it is one I have experienced personally. Instead of becoming the endpoint of thought, AI can become a cognitive surface against which thought continues to develop.

I bring an idea. The AI responds. Its response causes me to question something. I reject part of what it says. Another part reminds me of an experience I hadn't connected before. I recombine those things, form another thought, challenge the machine again, and suddenly another relationship appears. At times the interaction itself helps sustain the cognitive state long enough that the connections again begin arriving faster than I can verbalize them.

The architecture becomes something more like:

Human thought → AI interaction → human questioning → AI response → human acceptance, rejection, and recombination → new human thought → further interaction → human synthesis.

In that architecture, the machine participates, sometimes substantially, but the human remains generative. I call this AI-Infused Generative Cognitive Flow.

The distinction is enormously important for children. AI can amplify cognition, or it can substitute for cognition. Often the interface looks almost identical from the outside.

Which raises another question: Who is actually doing the thinking?

Staying the Author of Your Own Mind

In a conversation with educator and AI thought leader Susan Adams, she introduced me to language that immediately connected with this problem: Cognitive Sovereignty. One of the ways she describes the idea is "staying the author of your own mind."

That becomes profoundly important when a machine can participate in our thinking with us. Can a learner recognize what they brought to an idea and what the machine introduced? Can they identify what they accepted, what they rejected, what caused them to change direction, and what connection they themselves made? Can they explain why they believe the resulting conclusion rather than merely pointing to the intelligence that generated some of its ingredients?

In simpler terms:

Where does my thinking end and the machine's begin?

Generative Cognitive Flow without Cognitive Sovereignty creates an obvious danger: what feels like a highly productive cognitive experience may actually become sophisticated cognitive substitution. But Cognitive Sovereignty without generative thought isn't enough either. Protecting the authorship of your mind matters because your mind needs to be capable of producing consequential thought worth authoring.

The opportunity is to put the two together: use AI to amplify cognition rather than substitute for it, while developing enough metacognitive awareness to remain sovereign over the resulting thought and authentically participate in creating whatever emerges.

There is another idea downstream from this that deserves substantial attention: Provenance of Cognitive Sovereignty. If a learner claims authorship over AI-assisted thinking, can they demonstrate the path through which that thinking developed—what they brought, what the machine contributed, what they rejected, and where their own synthesis occurred?

That is an important question, but not the one this paper is trying to answer. For now, it is enough to recognize that our children won't merely need access to intelligence. They will need to know how to think with intelligence without surrendering the authorship of their own minds.

An Interesting Question Raised by Alpha School

One reason I have become interested in Alpha School is not because I believe it proves any of this. It doesn't. What interests me is the learning architecture.

Alpha has received substantial attention for an educational model in which students spend concentrated portions of their day engaged in individualized, technology-supported academic learning rather than spending the entire day in a conventional teacher-led classroom. The model appears to create relatively long, durable moments of individualized and isolated cognition under conditions quite different from the pacing, transitions, interruptions, social dynamics, and competing demands present in a traditional classroom.

Alpha reports striking outcomes from its model, although its reported outcomes, independently established evidence, and any causal explanations need to be distinguished carefully. I don't know whether Generative Cognitive Flow explains any portion of those outcomes.

But the environment raises an interesting question: if children repeatedly receive long, durable moments of relatively isolated cognition, might some of them be getting regular access to cognitive-flow states that are simply harder to sustain in many conventional environments? And if so, could repeated access to those states be one contributor to some of the confidence, independence, articulation, or learning behaviors people find compelling when observing those students?

I don't know, but I think it's a question worth asking.

The implication isn't that every child should attend Alpha School. It is that perhaps we should begin examining learning environments through an additional lens: Does this environment provide meaningful opportunities for sustained generative cognition?

A conventional classroom has enormous social and educational value, but it also has unavoidable interruptions. Children move at different speeds. Teachers have many students to serve. There are transitions, behavioral needs, social dynamics, and a schedule that eventually says it is time to stop thinking about one thing and begin thinking about another.

A child may actually be cognitively ready to enter deeper thought and have that state repeatedly broken by the pace, needs, behavior, or interruptions of other people in the room. That isn't an indictment of those children or their teachers. It is a structural characteristic of trying to educate many different human beings together.

But if repeated experiences of generative cognition matter, then uninterrupted cognitive runway matters too. A child may be ready to go somewhere deeper and simply not have enough runway to get there.

That becomes something worth noticing when we design and choose learning environments.

Back to Judgment

We began with a parent wondering what their child should develop for an AI-infused future. The answer I offered was judgment. We can now make the pathway underneath that answer more visible.

Knowledge and experience provide the material from which judgment can emerge. Sustained attention provides some of the conditions required to work with that material. Generative Cognitive Flow exercises the synthesis through which relationships become visible and new thoughts emerge. Metacognition allows us to observe and understand our own thinking. Cognitive Sovereignty becomes increasingly important when machine intelligence enters that process because it helps preserve human authorship over the resulting thought.

Repeated experiences of synthesis may strengthen some of the capacities upon which sophisticated judgment depends, and judgment allows a person to take information, experience, context, uncertainty, and increasingly machine intelligence and create a consequential human result from them.

That result matters for another reason. Human beings derive satisfaction not simply from being busy but from being capable of making a contribution that matters to somebody else. I have described this elsewhere as recognized contribution: the experience of doing something consequential enough that another person or community recognizes its value.

That matters to the child, not merely to the labor market. A young person who develops the ability to synthesize, form judgments, and turn those judgments into consequential actions or artifacts possesses a pathway through which their own thinking can become valuable to other human beings. Recognized contribution can become part of how competence develops into identity and how meaningful participation produces satisfaction.

That pathway can extend:

Generative Cognitive Flow → synthesis → judgment → consequential action or artifact → recognized contribution → identity and satisfaction.

There is also a second pathway emerging specifically because of AI:

Generative Cognitive Flow → metacognition → Cognitive Sovereignty → judgment over and with machine intelligence.

Those aren't proposed as complete scientific models of human development. They make visible relationships that deserve more attention. The contribution I am trying to explore isn't the claim that judgment, creativity, metacognition, or critical thinking are new. It is the relationship among them in the developmental environment now emerging around our children.

Could regular opportunities for Generative Cognitive Flow become one intentional developmental pathway through which children practice the synthesis, metacognition, and cognitive authorship increasingly required for judgment in an AI-infused world?

That is the proposition I believe deserves investigation, because judgment doesn't suddenly appear when someone turns eighteen, graduates from college, or enters a profession. If we expect our children to exercise sophisticated judgment in a world saturated with machine intelligence, we should care about the cognitive experiences through which its underlying capacities develop.

So What Should I Do for My Child?

This is where the temptation is to turn the argument into a list: five ways to create cognitive flow, ten minutes without a phone, a particular learning application, a prescribed amount of independent work.

That isn't what this paper is trying to do, because I don't think we've earned the right to be that prescriptive yet.

The first step is simpler: care about your child's capacity for sustained, generative thought.

Recognize it when you see it. Value it. Look for people and learning environments that value it too. Pay attention to whether your child merely receives information or regularly gets enough cognitive space to generate something of their own from it.

Begin the proactive pursuit of opportunities for your child to experience cognitive flow.

That doesn't mean we yet know precisely how to manufacture the state, how long it should last, how often it should occur, or what educational architecture best produces it. It means that once we recognize sustained generative cognition as developmentally important, we can begin treating its presence—or absence—as something worth noticing.

This is one rung in the ladder, not a complete answer to preparing a child for an AI-infused future.

Eventually we need to answer the harder questions: Where's the book on how to create judgment? Where's the book on getting daily doses of cognitive flow? How should parents, teachers, mentors, and schools deliberately create the conditions for it? What role should AI play? How do we know when AI is extending cognition and when it is replacing it? How might we cultivate Generative Cognitive Flow while preserving Cognitive Sovereignty?

Those questions deserve their own paper. This one intentionally stops here.

Its purpose is to make the case that before we prescribe the mechanics, we should recognize the capability and understand why it may matter.

A Capability Worth Looking For

We spend enormous energy asking what our children should learn in preparation for an AI-infused world. Perhaps an equally important question is:

What should their minds become capable of doing?

The AI models will change. The applications, interfaces, and occupations will change. Some of the skills we're frantically teaching today will become ordinary, automated, or obsolete. But our children will repeatedly encounter intelligence, information, recommendations, predictions, alternatives, and machine-generated answers that require something from them.

Judgment.

And if sophisticated judgment depends in meaningful part upon our capacity to remain with complexity, connect experiences, synthesize independent thoughts and learnings, generate new results, and remain authors of our conclusions, then those capacities deserve deliberate developmental attention.

The causal concern is straightforward. Our children are going to need judgment to remain meaningful contributors in a society increasingly infused with machine intelligence. Sophisticated judgment draws upon capacities for sustained cognition, synthesis, metacognition, and independent thought. Generative Cognitive Flow is a recognizable state in which those capacities are intensely exercised. Yet many of the natural opportunities children once had to remain with their own cognition are becoming scarcer at precisely the moment we should be paying more attention to cultivating them.

The capability may be becoming more important at precisely the moment the natural opportunities to develop it are becoming less common.

That is the dichotomy I find difficult to ignore.

We don't yet need every answer about how to reverse it. We can begin by recognizing the experience, valuing it when we see it, and looking for people and environments that understand it. We can become more deliberate about whether our children are regularly getting opportunities to enter it.

Perhaps, through repeated familiarity, something even more important can happen. Deep generative thought can cease to feel like an uncomfortable delay standing between a child and an answer. Cognitive flow can become a state they recognize, a state in which they are comfortable, and perhaps eventually a state they actually enjoy because they associate it with discovery, creation, and the satisfaction of producing something authentically their own.

That is where the proactive pursuit of cognitive flow by the adults around a child can eventually become the child's own persistent pursuit of cognitive flow.

If judgment will be among the capabilities that allow our children to remain meaningful contributors in an AI-infused society, then perhaps one of the most valuable things we can give them isn't another answer.

It is the ability—and eventually perhaps the desire—to stay with a difficult question long enough to create one of their own.

A daily dose of cognitive flow.
