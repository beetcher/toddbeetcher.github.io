BLACK FLAG

Harvesting the Old Economy to Own the New

An investment and business-building thesis for the AI-native economy

Black Flag intends to use its temporary advantage in AI-native software manufacturing to help established enterprises capture the financial benefits of an uncomfortable transition, then redeploy a portion of its own commercial profits—along with its manufacturing capability, Business Design, and intelligent systems—into building and owning interests in the next generation of businesses.

Executive Perspective

Every major technological revolution creates two opportunities: help existing businesses adapt to a new economic reality, and build the businesses that become possible because that reality has changed.

Artificial intelligence is creating both opportunities simultaneously.

Much of today’s attention is directed toward making individual employees more productive. Software developers receive AI coding assistants. Analysts produce research in minutes rather than days. These improvements matter, but they may represent only a fraction of the transformation underway.

AI is changing the means of producing software itself.

For decades, enterprises built expensive organizations around the assumption that software must be manufactured primarily through coordinated human labor. AI-native manufacturing challenges that assumption, with consequences for operating expenses, organizational structures, capital allocation, and enterprise value.

Black Flag sees a two-stage opportunity.

First, help established enterprises unlock economic value accumulated under the old means of software production. Rather than acquiring those companies, Black Flag can offer a financially compelling manufacturing alternative that allows existing leadership to capture the benefits of a more efficient operating model.

Second, redeploy a portion of Black Flag’s resulting commercial profits into creating and owning interests in new businesses built around AI-native economics from their inception.

Bending Spoons and other sophisticated operators have recognized opportunities to acquire established software businesses and improve their economics through operational transformation. Black Flag proposes a different approach: participate commercially in the transformation without acquiring the enterprise, then use part of the resulting profits to help finance ownership of tomorrow’s businesses.

1. The First Unlock: Accelerating Programmers Is Not Transforming Factories

To understand the magnitude of the opportunity, consider a hypothetical experiment set in 1989 at IBM Rochester, where enterprise software development depended on extensive human coordination.

Imagine a young programmer discovering a secret tool with the capabilities of today’s Claude Code.

An assignment that normally requires weeks can suddenly be completed in ten minutes. The programmer generates the code, checks it, and submits it.

But the surrounding organization hasn’t changed. His work still moves through reviews, meetings, integration, testing, correction, and release processes operating on human time.

The programmer has become extraordinarily productive. The enterprise has not.

Now imagine IBM management discovers the tool and gives it to every programmer.

Suddenly, everybody generates code in minutes. Productivity measurements improve dramatically. Management celebrates the breakthrough.

Yet projects still take months.

The programmers are finished, but the surrounding production organization cannot keep pace. Coordination, integration, testing, approvals, and organizational handoffs remain the constraints.

This is essentially where many enterprises find themselves today.

They have purchased enterprise access to tools such as Claude and are measuring improvements in coding productivity. They reasonably believe they have embraced the new technology.

But they may have accelerated only one component of a much larger production system.

The third stage of our experiment reveals the actual transformation.

What if the same machine-intensive approach could be applied not merely to writing code, but to orchestrating workflows, coordinating dependencies, integrating components, executing tests, identifying defects, making corrections, and preparing releases?

What if much of the production process itself moved from human time to machine time?

That is the distinction between AI-assisted programming and AI-native software manufacturing.

The breakthrough isn’t simply making programmers faster. It is making the factory keep up.

Human judgment remains essential. Someone must understand the customer, define the problem, establish requirements, evaluate tradeoffs, manage risk, and determine whether the resulting software creates business value.

But the economics of executing that judgment can change dramatically.

The opportunity is not merely to reduce the cost of writing software. It is to reconsider the organizational machinery enterprises constructed around the old cost of producing it.

That is a CEO, CFO, and board-level proposition.

2. The Second Unlock: Our Technological Advantage Has an Expiration Date

Black Flag Design has developed an AI-native software manufacturing capability intended to compress software production time and cost.

The commercial opportunity does not depend on possessing the world’s best factory indefinitely. It depends on having a sufficiently capable factory while many enterprises have not yet recognized the full implications of the technology.

Black Flag is not alone in recognizing these developments.

AI models, agentic programming systems, and automated production capabilities are advancing rapidly. Comparable manufacturing capabilities will become more accessible. Competition will increase. Pricing will face pressure.

Our working hypothesis is that the strongest differentiation window may be measured in a small number of years rather than decades, although its actual duration remains uncertain.

This creates the first strategic clock.

Black Flag must convert a temporary manufacturing advantage into commercial value while that advantage remains meaningful.

Technical improvement matters, but so do market intelligence, executive relationships, credible financial analysis, and speed.

The question is not simply how much better the factory can become. It is how much economic value Black Flag can help enterprises recognize and capture before the broader market catches up.

3. The Third Unlock: The Legacy Enterprise Opportunity Is a Finite Harvest

There is a second clock, and it may be even more important.

Today’s established enterprises were built over decades. Their software departments, management hierarchies, development practices, testing organizations, and integration processes evolved under one economic assumption: sophisticated software required large amounts of coordinated human labor.

That assumption shaped corporate spending and organizational design.

It also created enormous accumulated economic potential.

Consider an enterprise with hundreds or thousands of employees involved in software production. If a fundamentally different manufacturing model can deliver comparable or superior results at materially lower cost, the implications for profitability and enterprise value could be substantial.

Not every function can be automated. Institutional knowledge, security, regulatory obligations, integration, and business continuity impose real constraints.

Nevertheless, the potential magnitude warrants serious financial investigation.

Here is the uncomfortable part.

Capturing the full economic benefit may require enterprises to reconsider organizations that took decades to build.

Some roles will change. Departments may shrink. Management structures may be reorganized. Existing skills and practices may lose economic relevance.

For employees and leadership alike, these changes can be deeply uncomfortable.

But avoiding the question does not eliminate the underlying economic pressure. Corporate leadership must consider long-term competitiveness, financial sustainability, shareholders, customers, and employees. A materially more efficient means of production cannot be ignored indefinitely.

Black Flag’s role is not to decide whom an enterprise employs or how it restructures. Those decisions remain entirely with the customer.

Our role is to provide a credible economic alternative.

And the initial opportunity is not infinitely repeatable.

Once an enterprise transforms its software production economics, the same legacy cost structure is no longer available to transform again. An ongoing commercial relationship may remain, but the original economic discontinuity has largely been consumed.

Think of an orchard.

Decades of growth have produced trees bearing valuable fruit. Some will be harvested by competitors. Others will transform themselves. Eventually, the original crop will be gone.

Winter is coming.

Not because software will disappear, but because the unusual opportunity to capture value from the initial transition will diminish.

Together, the two clocks create urgency: our manufacturing advantage will become less distinctive, while the pool of untransformed enterprises will gradually shrink.

The strategic imperative is not simply to harvest this opportunity before it disappears, but to convert its proceeds into ownership of businesses positioned to thrive after the harvest is over.

4. The Fourth Unlock: There Is a Race for the Best Fruit

Several sophisticated businesses have recognized aspects of this opportunity.

Bending Spoons offers a useful example. Its acquisition-led model involves purchasing established software businesses and pursuing operational and financial improvements.

Black Flag sees a different path.

Rather than purchasing the enterprise, we propose partnering with its existing leadership.

The company retains its customers, brand, market position, and ownership. Black Flag supplies a new means of producing software.

The customer captures the resulting economic improvements. Black Flag earns revenue and potentially attractive profits from providing the manufacturing capability.

An acquisition strategy uses capital to obtain ownership of an existing business and capture the benefits of its transformation.

Black Flag’s commercial strategy seeks to participate through a customer relationship, without requiring the capital necessary to purchase the company.

Neither model is inherently superior in every situation. They carry different economics and risks. But both face incentives to move early.

For Black Flag, the immediate challenge is identifying enterprises most likely to recognize and act upon the opportunity.

That requires more than conventional software sales.

We need to understand where large software production expenses exist, which costs might be addressable, where management faces pressure to improve financial performance, and which leadership teams possess the authority and willingness to act.

This suggests an opportunity to build our own financial intelligence capability.

Using the factory itself, Black Flag could develop software that analyzes companies, models transformation economics, and helps experienced executives identify attractive prospects.

The technology would not replace executive judgment. It would extend its reach.

Two Markets, One Factory

There are two distinct categories of potential customers.

The first consists of enterprises with substantial existing software organizations.

Insurance companies, financial institutions, logistics businesses, manufacturers, and other software-dependent enterprises may have significant opportunities to improve their production economics.

For these customers, the proposition is principally financial: reduce the cost of producing software while preserving essential capabilities and business continuity.

The second consists of enterprises that historically could not justify building substantial software capabilities.

They may have wanted proprietary applications, automation, customer platforms, or other digital capabilities but found the cost and organizational burden prohibitive.

AI-native manufacturing can change that equation, making previously inaccessible capabilities economically practical.

Their proposition is not primarily workforce reduction. It is affordable access to new capabilities.

The same factory serves both markets, although the executive conversations differ.

One market seeks to transform an expensive existing capability. The other seeks to acquire a valuable new capability without inheriting the historical expense.

Both expand the commercial opportunity—and both can contribute to Black Flag’s larger ambition.

5. The Fifth Unlock: The Harvest Is Not the Destination

A successful enterprise manufacturing business would be valuable in its own right.

But it is not the full Black Flag thesis.

The more ambitious opportunity is to convert some of the profits generated during the transformation of existing enterprises into capital for creating new ones.

This is where Black Flag Design and Black Flag Ventures become complementary economic engines.

Black Flag Design is the commercial engine.

It supplies AI-native manufacturing to established enterprises, earns revenue, pays operating expenses, maintains necessary reserves, and seeks to generate available profit.

Black Flag Ventures is the venture-creation and ownership engine.

Through an agreed capital arrangement, a portion of available commercial profits could be directed toward identifying, validating, financing, and owning interests in new AI-native businesses.

Customer savings do not automatically become Black Flag revenue, and Black Flag revenue does not automatically become venture capital. The financial relationship between the two businesses must be deliberately structured.

But the underlying logic is powerful.

Use the value released by transforming established enterprises to finance businesses that never need to inherit those legacy structures.

An opportunity that once required millions of dollars and years of development may become testable with substantially less capital and time, where the application and manufacturing economics permit.

Historically, founders have often exchanged significant ownership for the capital required to build software before demonstrating meaningful market demand.

If Black Flag reduces the cost of reaching credible validation, it may reduce early financing requirements and preserve more ownership for the participants.

That does not eliminate investment risk or future dilution. It changes the starting economics.

From Venture Investing to Intelligent Venture Creation

Black Flag Ventures is designed to combine capital, Business Design, AI-native manufacturing, and intelligent systems into a repeatable process for identifying, validating, and developing new businesses—not simply investing in companies someone else has already created.

Its ambition is to combine capital with a repeatable system for creating and developing companies.

The operating concept is Qualify → Validate → Graduate.

Qualify promising opportunities through market, customer, financial, and competitive analysis.

Validate them through structured experiments, early customer evidence, and affordable software manufacturing.

Graduate the strongest opportunities toward independent operation, additional financing, strategic combinations, or other appropriate outcomes.

The system could become increasingly intelligent.

Software and AI-assisted analysis could compare opportunities, evaluate evidence, model capital requirements, monitor portfolio performance, and identify when ventures deserve additional resources.

Each venture would generate evidence about markets, customers, manufacturing costs, and business outcomes, creating a growing body of knowledge that could improve how Black Flag evaluates and develops subsequent opportunities.

Over time, Black Flag Ventures could recognize opportunities to combine complementary companies, redirect strategies, or discontinue investments that no longer justify their capital requirements, subject to appropriate ownership rights and governance.

The objective is not to automate investment judgment away. It is to give experienced business leaders a more capable system through which to exercise it.

What Endures After the Harvest?

A skeptical board member should ask: if AI-native manufacturing eventually becomes commonplace, what remains distinctive about Black Flag?

The answer cannot be that our factory will always be better.

It must be what we build while the advantage exists.

If the strategy succeeds, Black Flag Ventures could accumulate ownership interests in AI-native businesses, proprietary knowledge about venture outcomes, durable relationships with founders and institutions, and a repeatable system for allocating capital.

The factory may begin as the source of differentiation and eventually become widely accessible infrastructure.

The enduring value would need to reside elsewhere: ownership, business judgment, accumulated intelligence, relationships, and investment outcomes.

None of these advantages is guaranteed. Low-cost software does not create customer demand, and commercial success does not automatically produce successful investments.

But the architecture creates a plausible pathway from temporary technological differentiation toward a more durable economic position.

Without the second stage, Black Flag may become an excellent manufacturing business in an increasingly competitive market.

With it, Black Flag has an opportunity to build something that can outlast the initial advantage.

6. Why Black Flag—and What Must Be Proven

Executing this strategy requires complementary capabilities.

The factory provides the manufacturing foundation. Commercial leadership identifies enterprise opportunities. Financial and executive judgment connects manufacturing to board-level economic decisions. Business Design connects opportunities, customers, business models, validation, and venture development.

Black Flag is assembling these capabilities around a common thesis.

But recognizing an opportunity is not proof of execution.

Four questions will determine whether the model can become a substantial business.

Can the factory deliver repeatably? Its true costs, quality, reliability, integration requirements, and maintenance economics must be demonstrated.

Will enterprises purchase the proposition? Executive interest must translate into paid engagements and measurable customer benefits.

Can the commercial engine generate deployable capital? Revenue must become sustainable profit, with a workable mechanism connecting available capital to venture creation.

Can Black Flag Ventures invest that capital productively? Its qualification, validation, and portfolio-management system must demonstrate that it improves the economics of company creation and ownership.

Black Flag does not need to prove the entire thesis before beginning. It needs to validate critical assumptions in sequence, committing greater resources as evidence improves.

The first objective is commercial proof. The longer-term objective is a repeatable system for creating economic value and redeploying it intelligently.

Conclusion: Winter Is Coming. Plant the Future.

The old economy contains substantial value accumulated under decades of human-intensive software production.

AI-native manufacturing creates an opportunity to help established enterprises unlock that value, even when the transition requires uncomfortable organizational change.

Black Flag proposes to participate commercially in that transformation, while also bringing previously unaffordable software capabilities to enterprises that need them.

But the harvest is finite.

Manufacturing advantages will become more widely available. The most attractive legacy opportunities will be captured or transformed.

Rather than treating the harvest as the destination, Black Flag proposes to use a portion of its commercial profits to plant a new garden: AI-native businesses selected, financed, manufactured, and developed through an increasingly intelligent venture-building system.

The old economy provides the initial opportunity.

The new economy provides the long-term destination.

Help existing enterprises unlock the value of the new means of production. Capture a commercially justified share of that value. Consolidate a portion of the resulting profits. Then redeploy that capital intelligently into building and owning interests in the next generation of companies.

Others have recognized parts of this transformation. Black Flag’s opportunity is to connect the commercial and venture-creation consequences into one coherent economic system.

We do not need to own yesterday’s companies.

We want to help transform yesterday’s enterprises so we can help build and own tomorrow’s.

The harvest window is limited. What we plant with its proceeds could endure much longer.
