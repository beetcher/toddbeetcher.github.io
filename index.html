01 / THE WORKAn idea should become something you can inspect.

A new city shouldn’t
mean starting
from scratch.

A practical problem in expanding an AI workshop became a substantial application.

If an instructor becomes available in another city, where could people meet? Repeating venue research and data entry for every new territory would make expansion cumbersome.

I built a console that lets an operator choose a territory, specify the kinds of places to find, and use AI to propose candidates. Reviewed venues become permanent inventory the operation can use.

That discovery workflow connects to a larger system for people, classes, instructors, attendance, and communications. The important part is how the pieces work together.

AI suggestions arrive inactive. A person reviews them before activation. The system includes source tracking, prompt versions, run history, and spending controls.

02 / THE MOMENTThe same curiosity, much closer to home.

The light had been broken for years.
Then I thought to ask.

I’d lived with a dead dome light in our Honda. Then one day I thought: what does my AI assistant have to say about this? I could describe the car, show the assembly, and work out what I needed to understand.

That moment matters. Knowing AI exists is different from noticing when to reach for it.

I see the same possibility in a brisket made for friends, a story shared with Suzanne on a drive, and an operational problem at work. A useful personal experience can make someone want to try again.

That’s where I like to begin: help people notice a possibility that matters to them. Then help them carry that curiosity into their work.

03 / BECOMING CAPABLEThe person stays in charge.

One useful result.
Then a way to
begin again.

I want someone to leave thinking, “I know how to start figuring this out.”

The approach I’m developing starts with something a person wants to accomplish. We get oriented, try a manageable step, question what comes back, and improve it together.

AI can extend what someone can do. They still need to understand the decisions, check the result, and explain why it makes sense. Getting comfortable saying “I don’t know yet” is part of becoming capable.

  1. 01

    Get oriented.

    What are we trying to do? How does this environment work?

  2. 02

    Try something real.

    A small, meaningful step gives the next question somewhere to land.

  3. 03

    Question and improve.

    Check the answer. Notice what’s missing. Keep your judgment.

  4. 04

    Contribute. Repeat.

    Make something useful to somebody. Return with greater independence.

I’m developing this through learning experiences and a coaching pilot. The aim is capability that survives the session: reusable help, follow-up, and less need for someone else to supply every next step.

06 / STARTING HEREA beginning shaped around your organization.

First, I’d walk around
and meet people.

Before deciding what should change, I’d want to understand the people doing the work.

I’d listen to what they’re trying to accomplish, where work gets stuck, what they already use, and what makes them curious or uneasy. I’d learn with the managers and technical partners who know the organization.

Early in the week, I’d show examples from inside and outside work, then help people establish a usable AI environment within your tools and information boundaries.

I’d focus on recognizing opportunities and experiencing a useful first result. An end-of-week workshop could bring their questions and discoveries together and point toward worthwhile work applications.

Then we’d select a few opportunities, agree on owners and follow-up, and see what actually helps. The sequence would adapt to your organization; the first week is a launch, not a promise of transformation by Friday.

  1. CAPABILITY

    Can people recognize an opportunity, try it, and check the result?

  2. CONTINUED USE

    Do they return on their own? What still gets in the way?

  3. USEFUL WORK

    Does the chosen workflow improve—in quality, time, service, or another agreed measure?
