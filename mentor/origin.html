A Cowboy's Inheritance

The story behind The Roundup — and the family that made it possible.

Every parent I've ever known wants the same two things for their kids.

They want them to be satisfied. Not rich. Not famous. Not perfect. Satisfied — which is a word that covers everything worth covering without getting political about any of it.

And they want their kids to want to be with them. To stay close. To keep showing up for each other.

That's it. Two things. And they're both achievable.

I spent thirty years in technology, logged a million miles by the time I was thirty, and did everything I could to support the growth of two daughters. I watched Savannah play soccer in twenty-five different states before she graduated high school. I watched Brittney swim in thirty. Then COVID came and I had a moment — like a lot of people did — to stop and ask what the next chapter should be.

39 states. Two kids. A mom and a dad who showed up every time.

I knew I had at least one or two significant ones left. The question was what they should be worth.

I wanted the next one to connect the things that have always defined me: system-level thinking, a technology background, and a genuine belief that showing up for young people is the highest-leverage thing a person can do.

Then AI arrived. And I want to be specific about what happened next — because this is the whole point.

I hadn't programmed in thirty years. I didn't know what an IDE was — I thought it was a type of hard drive. I'd never heard of Python — I thought it was a snake. Without AI, building in today's stack means learning five foreign languages plus an entirely new working environment from scratch.

I didn't do that.

What I did was take the system-level thinking I've carried my whole life, point it at friction — and I can't stop seeing friction, it's just always there, I'll be doing something completely ordinary and see ten products that could be built — and I started building.

I built an iOS tracking application. I built AI-based tools across multiple substrates. I worked with vector embeddings. I built MVPs. Real ones. On my own. The only foundation I had was the basics I learned thirty years ago at IBM and Compaq.

Think of me as the first one through the door. No recent foundation. No current languages. Just system-level thinking — pointed at friction, used to train myself, and proven on my own work. I built real artifacts. I did it myself.

And now I want to teach others to do the same — so our children can be excited about their chapter, not afraid. So they can fully embrace the opportunity that's been handed to them, journey toward a satisfied life, and still want to come back and spend time with their family.

That's the goal. That's always been the goal.

The rest of this page is the story of why none of this surprised me — and the family that made it inevitable.

"Not land. Not money. Not possessions."

"A spirit. Passed forward through generations of people who showed up."

Fred.

Fred J. Beetcher — a relative by name and by blood — was 56 years old and working as a porter on the SS Edmund Fitzgerald when the ship went down in a November storm on Lake Superior in 1975. All 29 crew members were lost. His name is on the crew manifest. His name is on the memorial bell. His name is in the Gordon Lightfoot song that most of America knows by heart.

He shipped out, his son said later, when he got tired and wanted a change of pace. That's a cowboy. Still showing up. Still contributing. Right up until the very end.

Bud.

Milo "Bud" Saramba stormed the beaches of Normandy on D-Day. He walked across France. He made it all the way to Germany. He would tell his grandson stories about sitting in frozen rivers overnight, doing what had to be done. He came home.

He didn't come home and stop. He built a cabin by hand on a lake in the Northwoods. He planted gardens. He helped his neighbors. He built a church. And then he invested a decade of summers in that grandson — teaching him, without ever saying it directly, that a man's life is measured by what he builds and who he builds it for.

Elmer and the generation that came home.

Elmer served in the Navy in the Pacific during World War II. He and Bud were part of a generation of men who went away to the hardest thing imaginable and came home without making a show of it. They didn't talk about what they'd been through. They just kept building. Kept contributing. Kept showing up for the people around them. That was the whole point.

Bob.

Bob Beetcher was eighteen years old when he found out he was going to be a father. He didn't run. He worked through college while raising a family. He got a job at IBM and spent thirty years there — long enough to earn a full pension. Then he got another job and spent twenty-five more years. Two full careers. Fully committed. Both times.

On weekends, he built things. A garage. A finished basement. A sunroom. A trailer so his kid could go on bike rides. He didn't build because he had to. He built because that's what you do. You contribute. You make things better for the people around you.

You don't stop.

Todd.

I didn't storm any beaches. I didn't build a cabin by hand.

But something took root anyway.

As a young adult I spent summers with my grandparents and came home and mowed lawns for older neighbors on my street. Not for money. Because somebody needed it done. I coached soccer for years. I managed teams. I was always the one who raised his hand when a group of kids needed a driver, an organizer, an adult who would just show up.

When Savannah's college soccer team came to Colorado, I drove dinner from Boulder to Colorado Springs — two hours each way — just to feed the kids. When Brittney needed her car in Buffalo, I drove it to her from Boulder. I have a thousand versions of that story. None of them felt like sacrifice. They all just felt like what you do.

In 1997, I originated the concept behind the Cyrix WebPAD — a tablet computer that predated the iPad by more than a decade. I raced the Ironman World Championship in Kona — 140.6 miles — and finished ahead of more than a third of the professional field using a system-level approach I built from scratch. I finish what I start. I always have.

Then COVID came and I found myself on a glide path. Not lazy. Just unclear. I volunteered as a CDL bus driver because I needed to stay useful. Tending my own fire alone has never been in my nature.

Then AI arrived. And something lit up.

I had spent a lifetime building the exact skills the next generation needs right now — system-level thinking, ecosystem strategy, knowing how to take an idea all the way to something real. And I'd spent a lifetime showing up for young people in whatever way the moment called for. The Roundup isn't a new direction. It's the same direction I've always been heading.

Savannah and Brittney.

They didn't inherit land either. They got the same thing I got.

Savannah earned her doctorate in physical therapy. Brittney swam eight All-American performances and then went and got her law degree. She's 26. They're both in strong, healthy partnerships — building their own lives, growing into the next chapter.

They still call. They still show up. The chain didn't break — it got stronger.

That's not parenting luck. That's an inheritance doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

Constantly contributing to your community. That was the cowboy way. It's also mine.

And right now, the most useful contribution I can make is this — helping a new generation of builders learn to think at the system level and embrace AI as the most powerful tool ever handed to them.

That's the skill. That's the match. That's why I showed up.

The Inheritance.

Fred showed up on a ship in November.

Bud showed up on a beach in France.

Elmer showed up in the Pacific.

Bob showed up every single day for fifty-five years — and then went home and built something.

Todd showed up for other people's kids, for decades — and then built something new.

Savannah and Brittney showed up. The chain continues.

None of them ever stopped contributing. Not one of them. That's not a coincidence. That's an inheritance. Not of land or money — but of a way of being in the world. A belief that your life is measured by what you build and who you build it for. That the community around you is better because you showed up.

If you've read this far — maybe you inherited a little of it too.
