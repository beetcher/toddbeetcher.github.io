# Judgment and critical thinking: design note (PLAN-R5)

**Status: DRAFT for Todd's review. Practice and discussion material only. It is not a validated psychometric instrument. It does not measure intelligence, honesty, or future job performance, and it makes no claim of equivalence to any published appraisal.**

## The problem
Facts have an answer key. Judgment mostly does not: two careful people can look at the same messy situation and weigh it differently. A pool built like trivia would either force one "right" answer onto a contested situation or quietly reward one personality or background. So the items here are built differently.

## Approach
1. **Situational items.** Each question is a short scenario with invented, neutral details (a shop, a school club, a small team, a shared spreadsheet). The choices are courses of action or interpretations, not facts.
2. **READY's three roles carry the nuance.** This is the main reason READY fits the problem, because the role lives on the connection between an answer and one set, not on the answer itself.
   - `strong`: the most defensible response in this context, and the feedback says why it holds up.
   - `ambiguous`: a reasonable response with a real trade-off or hidden cost. The feedback names the cost and does not punish it. A connection note tells the editor why it is ambiguous. These choices show how a person weighs trade-offs.
   - `incorrect`: clearly worse: it acts on weak evidence, skips an obvious check, or contains a reasoning error. The feedback says which, in plain words.
3. **Question types.** `single_choice` for "most defensible response". `multiple_choice` for "choose the two best responses" (two strong, plus other options that are ambiguous or incorrect).
4. **Difficulty is ambiguity and the number of competing considerations, not vocabulary or domain knowledge.** Introductory: one clear consideration. Basic: one main consideration plus a tempting shortcut. Intermediate: two considerations that pull apart. Advanced: several considerations, some information missing. Expert: incomplete or conflicting information, where the best move may be to narrow the uncertainty first. No item needs specialist knowledge.
5. **Tags by skill** (`skill_*`) so tests can be assembled per skill later, plus `format_*`, an optional `move_ask_or_delay` tag where the strong move is to ask or wait, and `scenario_*` tags where several decision points share one scenario.
6. **Shared scenarios.** About a dozen scenarios are reused with different decision points (for example, a spreadsheet figure that looks wrong; a rumor that a supplier is closing). One scenario then serves different skills and different audiences. Each question still repeats its scenario in full so every question atom stands alone.
7. **Reuse of answer atoms.** Many answers are general moves ("Ask where the figure came from before using it"). The same wording can be strong where information is missing, ambiguous where time is short, and incorrect where the question has already been settled. That is the point of contextual roles.

## Rules the content follows
- No political, religious, partisan or culture-war scenarios; nothing that depends on one country's or culture's norms; nothing that rewards an ideology, personality type or social background.
- Feedback explains what the person is weighing, why the strong choice holds up, what each other choice costs, and how to reduce the uncertainty. Tone: curious, not "gotcha". Reasoning errors are named by what they do ("using the conclusion as its own proof") and the common label is given second.
- At every level some items have "ask for more information" or "delay the decision" as the strong move.
- Check applied to every item: would two thoughtful people disagree about the `strong` choice? If yes, the item was rewritten or the contested choice moved to `ambiguous`.

## Limits (be honest about them)
- Even a well-built item has judgment calls inside it. The "strong" choice is the most defensible one under the stated facts, not the only acceptable one in life.
- A learner can learn the pattern ("always ask for more information") and game it. Items therefore include cases where asking is too slow, or the information is already on the table, so asking is an `ambiguous` or `incorrect` choice there.
- Scores from these items should not be used to rank, hire, or judge people. Use them to practise and to start discussions.
- All content is original draft material awaiting an owner's review, including the wording, the roles and the difficulty labels.
