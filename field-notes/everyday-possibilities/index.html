Everyday field notes

The moment you think to ask.

Everyday examples of turning unfamiliarity into something useful for the people around me.

Todd Beetcher

The moment that interests me is often small: I’m doing something ordinary, notice a problem, and remember that I can ask for help understanding it.

The light I had stopped noticing.

The dome light had been out for years. Instead of continuing to live with it, I described the car and the problem, asked what to buy, and worked out the installation. The important change was thinking to ask in the first place.

Learn the world around the brisket.

Cooking a brisket meant understanding the cut, the smoker, temperature, timing, and the stall. I could ask for the advice an experienced cook would give an apprentice, question what I was missing, and try again. The result mattered because I was making something for people I care about.

Something we could enjoy together.

On a drive through the San Luis Valley, I asked my assistant to tell us the valley’s story. Suzanne wanted more. A capability I had learned became a shared experience on the road.

The point is the next moment.

These are my personal experiences, not participant testimonials. They explain why I want someone else to have a useful first experience and begin noticing opportunities of their own. At work, that recognition needs to connect to approved tools, appropriate information, and a real task worth improving.
